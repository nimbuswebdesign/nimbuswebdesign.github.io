Why your Wix site isn't showing up on Google.

Nine times in ten it is one of six settings rather than anything structural: the site was never published to the real domain, a page is switched to hidden from search, every page shares one title, the sitemap was never submitted, the content only appears once scripts run, or the site is simply too new. All six are checkable in about fifteen minutes.

First, the fair part: Wix is not the reason your site cannot be found. It gets blamed for a lot that is really a settings problem, and modern Wix can produce a perfectly findable site. It serves real HTML for most content, generates a sitemap for you, and exposes per-page titles and descriptions.

What it also does is ship defaults that are easy to leave alone, and hide a couple of switches that quietly remove a page from search. That is what this guide is about. Work down it in order, because the cheap checks are first on purpose.

1. Check you are looking at the right site

Search Google for site:yourdomain.com, using your actual domain with no www and no https. That returns roughly what Google has stored for you.

If it returns nothing, try site:yourname.wixsite.com as well. If results appear there instead, your content is indexed under the free Wix subdomain and your real domain is a separate, empty site as far as Google is concerned. Connecting the custom domain and confirming the site is published, not merely saved in the editor, fixes it, and nothing further down this list matters until it is fixed.

2. Look for the switch that hides a page from search

Wix has a per-page setting that removes a page from search engines. It lives in that page's SEO settings, it is one toggle, and it is very easy to leave on from when the site was being built.

Check it on every page, not only the home page. A common pattern is a site where the home page ranks and nothing else does, because the inside pages were built later with the toggle still set from a staging phase.

3. Read your own tab titles

Open each page and read the browser tab. If every page says the same thing, or says only your business name, search engines have nothing to tell your pages apart with and none of them will rank for anything specific.

Every page needs its own title describing that page, and its own description. In Wix these are per-page fields, and the default is often one site-wide value nobody changed. This is the highest-value hour you can spend on a Wix site.

4. Make sure Google has actually been told

Wix generates a sitemap at yourdomain.com/sitemap.xml. Open it in a browser. If it loads, confirm Google has it.

Set up Google Search Console for the domain, verify it, and submit that sitemap URL. Then run the URL Inspection tool on your home page. It will tell you plainly whether the page is indexed and usually why not. It is the most useful free tool in this entire subject and most small business owners have never opened it.

5. Check that your words are in the HTML

Right-click the page, choose View Page Source, and use Ctrl+F to search for a sentence you can see on screen. If you find it, crawlers can read it. If you cannot, that text is being drawn by JavaScript after the page loads.

Google will usually still render it, but on a separate and later queue, and most AI crawlers and link previews do not run scripts at all. That matters more every year as answer engines take a larger share of the search surface. If your key content fails this test, it is worth knowing before concluding the problem lies elsewhere.

6. Consider that the honest answer might be time

A domain a few weeks old with no other sites linking to it will not rank for competitive commercial searches, whatever it is built on. That is not a Wix problem and rebuilding will not help it.

If your site is genuinely findable by the checks above and simply has not ranked yet, the useful work is elsewhere: your Google Business Profile, your reviews, and being mentioned on other sites. For a local business those three outrank almost anything you can do to the website itself.

When leaving Wix is actually the right answer

Rarely, and not for the reasons usually given. If the six checks come back clean and your pages are indexed, changing platforms will not improve your rankings and you will spend money to stand still.

The genuine reasons to move are different: you need a page structure the editor will not give you, your pages are heavy enough to fail on mobile data and you cannot slim them, or you want to own the files and change host without rebuilding. Those are real. Wix is bad for SEO, as a blanket statement, is not.

Common questions

Is Wix bad for SEO?

Not inherently. Wix exposes titles, descriptions, canonical behaviour and a sitemap, which is most of what matters. What it does do is make it easy to leave defaults in place, and it hides one toggle that removes a page from search entirely. Most Wix sites that cannot be found have a settings problem rather than a platform problem.

How long before a new Wix site appears on Google?

Indexing can happen within days once the sitemap is submitted and the pages are crawlable. Ranking for competitive commercial searches takes months, and depends far more on your profile, reviews and links than on the site.

Will moving off Wix make me rank better?

Only if a platform limitation is genuinely what holds you back, which is less common than it is claimed to be. Run the six checks first. If they come back clean, a rebuild costs money and changes nothing.

Why does only my home page show up?

Usually the hide-from-search toggle left on for inside pages, or every page sharing one title so Google treats them as the same thing. Check both before assuming anything more complicated.
