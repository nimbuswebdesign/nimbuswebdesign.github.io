Why your Squarespace site isn't showing up on Google.

Squarespace usually gets the basics right, so when a Squarespace site cannot be found the cause is normally different: a site-wide title format that makes every page look alike, automatically generated tag and category pages splitting your content, page weight on mobile, or the whole site still sitting behind a password.

Squarespace ships cleaner markup than it tends to get credit for. Your content is generally in the HTML, it produces a sitemap, and it handles canonical tags sensibly on its own. So the failure modes are not the ones people arrive expecting.

The problems below are the ones that actually turn up on Squarespace sites, roughly in the order worth checking.

1. Is the site still private?

Squarespace sites can be password protected or left unpublished while being built, and both are invisible to search engines. It sounds too obvious to be worth checking, and it is regularly the answer.

Search site:yourdomain.com. Nothing at all, on a site that has been live for months, points here first.

2. The site-wide title format problem

Squarespace builds page titles from a format you set once, typically Page Title followed by Site Title. That is fine until the page titles themselves are generic, and Services followed by your business name tells Google almost nothing. It is what most sites end up with.

The fix is per-page: give each page a title describing what is on it and where you work, and write its own description. On most Squarespace sites this is the highest-value change available and it costs nothing but an afternoon.

3. Tag and category pages splitting your content

If you use the blog or a portfolio, Squarespace generates archive pages automatically for every tag and category. On a site with a handful of posts and a dozen tags, that produces a lot of thin pages that look substantially like each other and like the posts they list.

Google clusters near-duplicates and picks one representative, so this can pull attention away from the pages you wanted to rank. If you are not deliberately using tags as navigation, use fewer of them.

4. Page weight, which is where Squarespace does struggle

Squarespace templates are visually rich, and that carries real weight: large hero images, several fonts, gallery scripts. On a phone on cell data is where the experience actually happens for a local business.

Run the page through PageSpeed Insights and read the mobile tab. If the largest item is an image measured in megabytes, resize it before doing anything else. Speed will not outrank relevance, but a slow page loses the visitor after they have already found you, which is worse.

5. Confirm Google has your sitemap

Squarespace publishes one at yourdomain.com/sitemap.xml automatically. Open it, verify the domain in Google Search Console, and submit it.

Then run URL Inspection on a page that is not appearing. Search Console will usually tell you directly whether the page is indexed and what is stopping it, which beats guessing.

Common questions

Is Squarespace bad for SEO?

No. It handles most of the technical layer for you, which is more than can be said for plenty of hand-built sites. Its weaknesses are page weight on mobile and the thin archive pages it generates automatically, not the fundamentals.

Should I delete my Squarespace tag pages?

If they exist only because you tagged posts and nobody navigates by them, using fewer tags is usually an improvement. If they are genuine navigation that visitors use, keep them and give each one real content at the top.

My Squarespace site is slow on phones. Is that why I am not ranking?

Probably not the main reason, but it is costing you enquiries after the click, which matters more. Speed is a modest ranking input and a large conversion one. Fix the images first; on Squarespace that is most of it.

Why city pages usually backfire

A city page should answer a real local need with useful, distinct information. Near-duplicate pages can compete for the same intent, and pages made mainly to funnel similar searches can violate Google's doorway-abuse policy.
