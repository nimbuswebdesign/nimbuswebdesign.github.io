What a contractor's website actually needs.

For most trades businesses the website is not how customers find you — the Google Business Profile is. The site's job is to convince somebody who has already found you, and to answer the questions that decide whether they ask for a quote.

Almost every contractor site I look at is built on the wrong assumption: that the site is the front door. For a local trade it usually isn't. People search, they see the map results, they read the reviews, and only then do they click through to see whether you look like someone who will turn up and do a tidy job.

That changes what the site is for. It is not a brochure and it is not a portfolio for other designers to admire. It is the thing that turns a maybe into a phone call, and there are about six things that decide whether it does.

The profile finds you. The site convinces them.

In the local results, most of what decides your position sits in the Business Profile rather than the website — the category you have chosen, the reviews, whether the profile is complete and active. The site contributes, but it is not the main lever, and a beautiful site attached to a neglected profile will lose to a plain site attached to a good one.

So the order matters. Fill the profile in properly first. Then make the website good at the job that is genuinely its own: proving you are real, competent and worth calling.

Your photographs are the product

Nobody is buying a service they can inspect in advance. They are buying evidence that your last twenty jobs went well. Photographs of real work do more of that than any amount of copy, and stock photography actively hurts — people recognize it, and recognising it costs you the trust the page was trying to build.

Two habits are worth more than a photographer. Take a wide shot of every job before you start, from the same spot you will stand for the after shot. And photograph the boring things: the prep, the masking, the tidy site at the end of the day. Finished-state photos all look alike. The prep is what separates you from the cheapest quote.

Say what you do and where, once, in plain words

A contractor site needs to answer two questions in the first few seconds: what exactly do you do, and do you come to me. Most fail the first by listing everything, and fail the second by burying the area in the footer.

What it does not need is a page per town. Generating fifty near-identical pages with the place name swapped is still the most common thing sold as local SEO, and it reliably makes a small site perform worse than it did before. If you serve twenty towns, say so in a sentence on a page that reads like a person wrote it.

It will be opened on a phone, outdoors, on cell data

Your customer is standing in their driveway comparing three quotes, on a phone, on a patchy connection. That is the real testing environment, and it is nothing like the desktop the site was designed on.

Practically: the phone number should be tappable and visible without scrolling, the images should be sized for a phone rather than shrunk in the browser, and the page should be useful before every script has finished loading. Most slow contractor sites are slow for one reason — unoptimized photographs straight off a camera.

Answer the questions that decide the quote

The pages that earn traffic and calls are the ones answering what people actually type before they buy: what it costs, how long it takes, what happens on the day, how to compare two quotes that look very different.

Contractors avoid the cost question because they cannot give a number without seeing the job. That is true, and it is not a reason to say nothing. Explaining what moves the price — condition, access, prep, how many coats — is more useful than a number, and it is the explanation you are already giving on driveways.

Have your license and insurance ready to show

This one is specific and it costs work when it is missing. In deed-restricted communities, an architectural review application commonly has to include the contractor's license and certificate of insurance before the homeowner is allowed to start. If producing those takes you three days, you are the reason their application missed the committee meeting.

Whether or not the numbers go on the website is a judgment call — an advertised warranty or coverage figure is an enforceable claim, so it should only ever be published once it is true and current. But the documents themselves should be somewhere you can send in a minute.

Common questions

Do I even need a website if my Business Profile is good?

You need one, but perhaps not the one you were imagining. The profile does the finding; people still click through to check you are real before they call. A small, fast, honest site does that job. A large one does not do it better.

Should I list every town I serve?

List them in a sentence, on a normal page. Do not build a page per town. Google clusters near-identical pages and ignores most of them, and the site-wide quality signal drags down the pages that were genuinely good.

How many photos is enough?

Enough to show range and consistency — usually a dozen or two, covering different job types. Quality of subject matters more than quality of camera. A clear phone photo of real work beats a professional shot of somebody else's.

Should I put prices on it?

Only if you can stand behind them. What works better for most trades is explaining what drives the price, which helps somebody compare three quotes honestly and costs you nothing you cannot deliver.

What is the single highest-value thing I can do this week?

Text every customer whose job you finished with a link to leave a review. Review count and recency are among the strongest things you control in local search, and no amount of website work substitutes for them.

More guides

Is my website actually findable?

Start with a site:yourdomain.com search, then confirm individual pages in Search Console. Search results are a useful spot check, but they are not a complete inventory of indexed pages.

Why your Google Business Profile beats your website

Google says local results depend mainly on relevance, distance and prominence. Accurate business details and genuine reviews help customers assess you; Google does not publish fixed percentage weights or guarantee a position.

Why city pages usually backfire

A city page should answer a real local need with useful, distinct information. Near-duplicate pages can compete for the same intent, and pages made mainly to funnel similar searches can violate Google's doorway-abuse policy.
