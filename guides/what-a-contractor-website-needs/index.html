Guide

A contractor website checklist for better inquiries.

A useful contractor website explains the work you do, where you work, shows real project evidence and makes requesting an estimate easy. Check those basics before paying for more pages or advertising.

For painters, roofers, landscapers and other service businesses, a customer might arrive from Google Maps, an ordinary search result, a recommendation or a sign on a truck. The website should help that person decide whether your business suits the job.

Open your site on a phone and work through these six checks. Write down the page address, what you tried and anything that made the next step difficult. This is a practical review, not a score that predicts Google rankings.

1. Can someone tell what you do and where?

Read the first screen without using the menu. It should name your main service and the area you actually cover. A visitor should not have to search the footer to learn whether you come to Lakewood Ranch, Sarasota or Bradenton.

Keep the service list aligned with the work you want. If cabinets are an occasional painting job, they should not displace interior, exterior or commercial painting as the main offer. Create separate pages when the service needs a distinct explanation; a simple offer may fit on one page.

2. Can they see relevant work you really completed?

Choose photos you have permission to publish. Explain the type of project, what needed attention and the work performed. Before-and-after images are most useful when the angle and lighting let someone make a fair comparison.

Show preparation and finished details where they help a customer understand the job. Label concept images and family projects honestly. Do not imply a customer relationship, qualification or result that you cannot support, and keep private addresses and identifying details out unless approved.

3. Is the estimate request easy on a phone?

Find the call button and quote request from the homepage and a service page. Check that the phone number is correct and that labels explain what happens next. If a button opens an email app, say so and display the email address as an alternative.

For a form, ask only for information needed to respond: contact details, job type, location and a short description. Test required fields and the error message. Use an authorized test submission to check receipt in the actual inbox or dashboard; a thank-you message alone does not prove the right person received it.

4. Does the page answer the questions behind the quote?

Explain what changes the price and what is included. For painting, useful details can include surface condition, repairs, access, preparation and the requested finish. Publish fixed prices only for a scope you can honor.

Describe how the estimate and scheduling work, who supplies materials and how feedback or changes are handled. Use the business’s actual process. A clear answer can help a suitable customer inquire without committing you to an unverified price or turnaround.

5. Can people and Google reach the important pages?

Follow the menu and service links. Look for missing pages, oversized photos, overlapping text and contact actions that disappear on a phone. A readable page should not depend on an animation finishing.

Use Google Search Console to inspect an important page and see whether it is indexed. Review search queries and landing pages over a stated date range. A sitemap helps discovery, but submitting one does not guarantee indexing or a particular position. Keep your Google Business Profile services, hours and contact details accurate too.

6. Are inquiries becoming suitable jobs?

Keep a simple record of received inquiries: date, requested service, town, source when known, estimate sent and won or lost. Leave information blank when you do not know it. Search impressions and phone-button clicks are different from received inquiries.

If suitable people visit but do not inquire, review the offer, proof and contact path. If almost nobody sees the site, investigate search visibility and referral sources. If inquiries are mostly for work you do not want, adjust how the main services are presented before buying more traffic.

Common questions

Do I need separate pages for every town?

No. State the real service area clearly. A separate location page makes sense when it contains useful, distinct information for customers there. Swapping a town name into near-identical pages adds little value and can create search-quality problems.

Can a one-page contractor website work?

Yes, for a simple offer with enough space to explain the service, show relevant work and provide contact details. Separate service or project pages can help when customers need more information. Choose the scope around the business, not a promised ranking benefit from page count.

How should I ask for reviews?

Invite customers with genuine experience to leave honest feedback using your Google review link. Do not offer rewards, request only positive reviews or discourage negative ones. Follow the communication permissions and preferences that apply to each customer.

Is my website actually findable?

Start with a site:yourdomain.com search, then confirm individual pages in Search Console. Search results are a useful spot check, but they are not a complete inventory of indexed pages.

Why your Google Business Profile beats your website

Google says local results depend mainly on relevance, distance and prominence. Accurate business details and genuine reviews help customers assess you; Google does not publish fixed percentage weights or guarantee a position.

Why city pages usually backfire

A city page should answer a real local need with useful, distinct information. Near-duplicate pages can compete for the same intent, and pages made mainly to funnel similar searches can violate Google's doorway-abuse policy.
